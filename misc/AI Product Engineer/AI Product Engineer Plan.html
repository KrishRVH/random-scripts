01The role this plan develops

Own the decisions around the code

Learn to use agents to engineer software and to build products whose behavior depends on models. Delegate implementation and routine checks while owning the problem definition, acceptance criteria, permissions, and operating decisions. Use SVPG's four risks to assess the result, checking it against users and the domain.

Risk 01 · Value

Will anyone choose this?

A real user chooses it over the workaround they have today.

Risk 02 · Usability

Can they figure it out?

They finish the task without you there to explain it.

Risk 03 · Feasibility

Can the team build and operate it?

The team can ship and operate it under real constraints, including failure.

Risk 04 · Viability

Does it work for the business?

It fits the strategy, costs, legal duties, and organizational limits.

At completion you can
  • 01Discover and validate a meaningful user problem.
  • 02Design a polished, accessible end-to-end experience.
  • 03Model the domain with users, and carry its language and rules into code and tests.
  • 04Architect and operate an evolvable production system.
  • 05Build agentic capability with tools, evals, security, and observability.
  • 06Measure outcomes: show how the shipped code changed behavior.
No curriculum can guarantee job security. This plan concentrates on work that is harder to commoditize: deciding, integrating, evaluating, and taking responsibility for outcomes.

Direct and review the agent's work

Make a brief prediction before consulting an agent's answer, spending up to 20 minutes on the judgment you most need to practice. Use code and primary sources to check your reasoning. This is a learning exercise; in product work, choose the division of investigation, planning, and implementation that the task needs.

Give the agent the intended outcome, constraints, allowed actions, and required evidence. Inspect changed boundaries and behavior with serious consequences directly. 04Four practitioner case studies

Learn to direct work you can trust

Study how Lauren Tan, Kun Chen, Jeffrey Emanuel, and Steve Yegge structure agent work. Apply their practices to your own product: define its rules, constrain authority, verify behavior, and recover when execution stops. Do each exercise in the phase that teaches the skills it requires.

Lauren Tan / poteto · Domain rules and verification+

Give agents context and a way to check their work

During the weekly build block, use Tan's pstack techniques to give the agent a clear outcome and room to explore solutions. Require evidence from the running product before accepting its work.

Make verification repeatable

  • Use the existing test runner, API, or browser driver to launch the app, seed data, use test identities, drive interactions, and collect screenshots and runtime traces. Add a small control CLI where a capability is missing.
  • Provide subcommands that work together, useful --help, machine-readable output, errors that explain what to do next, and --dry-run for destructive operations.
  • Keep a feature map beside the verification skill: what each feature does, how users reach it, and how the agent can exercise it. Maintain the map with the app.
  • In pstack, use /create-verification-skill to build the project-specific skill and /maintain-verification-skill to maintain it. Tan recommends a daily maintenance pass. Establish reliable verification before scaling parallel work.
The Complete Guide to pstack Pt. 1

Resolve uncertainty before implementation

  • Ask the agent to restate the underlying problem before proposing your own solution. Correct misunderstandings while leaving implementation choices open.
  • Use /how to trace behavior, /why to investigate rationale, /recall to recover relevant work, and /teach to explain the reasoning and tradeoffs in terms you can assess.
  • For a shared package, start with a caller's usage example or tutorial, then sketch types and public signatures. Keep tutorials, how-to guides, reference, and explanation distinct with /technical-writing.
  • Prototype competing approaches under the same constraints and compare their behavior. For larger architectural boundaries, use /architect: establish the problem, compare design sketches with an independent model, and implement the strongest design. Rework it if runtime evidence or repeated caller workarounds expose a flaw.
The Complete Guide to pstack Pt. 2
For the weekly exercise, choose one uncertainty in the slice and resolve it with a runnable experiment. Give the agent a repeatable check, then review the resulting evidence alongside the change.
Practice in Phases 2-5. Test four things on the same product:
  • Write an invalid domain example. Enforce its rule in a type, constructor, API, or runtime check, then ask a fresh agent to extend the design.
  • Show a defect the verifier catches and one it cannot. Check authorization, persisted state, and concurrent effects directly when they matter.
  • Compare two design sketches, divide verification coverage, and commission a fresh review. Explain what each exercise establishes and why model agreement is not proof.
  • Trace a recurring defect to its cause. Add a narrow check, test it against a legitimate case, and remove a stale instruction or unnecessary wrapper.
Kun Chen · Authority and supervision+

Firstmate uses one liaison to supervise workers in isolated worktrees, with durable task records and event-driven monitoring. Scout tasks cover investigation; ship tasks authorize delivery.

Study the boundaries

Learn decision rights, Git isolation, reproducible verification, model selection, and failure detection. A worktree isolates files; shared databases, credentials, and deployment targets need their own controls.

Practice in Phases 4-5

  • Write investigation and implementation briefs for the same issue. Name the allowed actions, stopping condition, evidence, and person who can authorize release.
  • Review an isolated change and record the checked revision. Change it and require fresh evidence before acceptance.
  • Compare two eligible models on the same ordinary tasks. Record correctness, your review time, elapsed time, and cost. When quota runs out, keep the same capability requirements for any replacement model.
  • Supervise two workers and interrupt one. Check what it actually completed before restarting it. Save a record of the owner, current state, and evidence for the next safe action. Explain when direct supervision is simpler than adding a liaison.
Firstmate · no-mistakes · quota-axi
Jeffrey Emanuel · Plans that survive the conversation+

The Flywheel separates planning from self-contained execution tasks. Beads Rust stores tasks and dependencies; Beads Viewer helps inspect the graph and select ready work.

Study the dependencies

Learn specification, interface design, dependency graphs, source checking, and automation economics. Use the task graph to find blockers and product evidence to decide what deserves priority.

Practice in Phases 3-5

  • Describe the user, constraints, invariants, and unknowns. Compare a competing plan and resolve one uncertainty with a prototype.
  • Split a change into four to eight restartable tasks. Include context, dependencies, affected interfaces, tests, and completion evidence. Give one to a fresh worker with no preceding chat.
  • Remove a false dependency and find a missing one. Include a deliberately stale claim in a handoff exercise and check it against the running system or source. Save the corrected dependency graph and the evidence that resolved the claim.
  • Turn a repeated inspection into a rerunnable command. Compare its total cost, including upkeep, with the manual procedure.
The Complete Flywheel Guide · Beads Rust · Beads Viewer
Steve Yegge · Continuity and recovery+

Yegge describes persistent operational roles, monitors that wake models when judgment is needed, and handoffs between temporary sessions. Practice session handoffs and recovery with one bounded local role. These exercises require no assumptions about model welfare or his forecasts.

Study what survives a restart

Learn durable state, ownership, cancellation, idempotency, monitoring, and backpressure. A timeout means the observer stopped waiting; the worker may still be running.

Practice in Phases 4-5

  • Define a role by its purpose, permissions, durable state, human owner, and escalation path. Resume it in a fresh session and verify its work.
  • Write a deterministic monitor that calls a model only for an ambiguous event. Test duplicate events, false alarms, and hostile log text. Authorize writes separately.
  • Interrupt a job after a partial effect. Record completed work, pending effects, and the next safe action. Recover without repeating the effect or overwriting another worker's work.
  • Inject a recoverable failure or integration conflict. Trace the cause, choose rollback or repair, and limit incoming work when verification cannot keep up.
The Shape of Things to Come · Model Welfare for Agentic Engineers
Use your existing tools or a local simulation to test these practices. Keep a practice when its results justify the complexity, supervision, and cost. Use one product for all four case studies and their exercises.

06The track · 72 weeks

Six phases, split in two

Start with human and product foundations in Part I, then develop technical judgment and agentic engineering in Part II. Carry one product through the phases. The 72 scheduled weeks total 648 hours at nine hours per week. If a pass condition needs more work, repeat its practice and check, adding nine hours per week. Allow one repeat block per phase and up to eight additional weeks across the course. If you still cannot meet the pass condition, get targeted feedback and revise the exercise. Demonstrate the required judgment before moving on.

Pass with your own reasoning

Before consulting an agent's answer, write your prediction, sketch, or diagnosis. Use the build and observation blocks to test it, then log what you missed and what assistance you needed. Start the phase's transfer check at least two weeks before its end. Solve one case, then a changed case 7-14 days later. Ask a colleague or agent to supply the changed case without its solution. Pass only when you can explain and verify the result yourself.

Interface design & visual execution

Make the interface feel intentional by grounding visual decisions in hierarchy. Give a reason beyond "it looks cleaner."
72 hrsWeeks 9-16

Teardown template
  • Intent · Attention · Interaction · State · Emotion
Six interface states
  • Normal: content is available and the user can perform the task.
  • Empty: no records or results are available. Explain why and offer a useful next step.
  • Loading: an operation is in progress. Show that it is underway and preserve context.
  • Error: an operation failed. Explain what happened and how to recover.
  • Success: an operation completed. Confirm the result and show what the user can do next.
  • Disabled: an action is unavailable because a prerequisite or permission is missing. Explain what is required.
Produce
Run preference tests only after users can complete the task.
For one workflow, compare two or three runnable variants on the same task. Capture screenshots or video, measure the interaction, and use the result to choose the design.
Extend the same AI task into a read-only interaction. Compare two context choices and two eligible models on cases kept out of prompt development. Show the supporting evidence and test missing or conflicting information, correction, abstention, and human fallback. Observe how users respond to a plausible mistake.
Pass condition

You explain every major visual decision in terms of hierarchy, comprehension, behavior, convention, or brand. In the transfer check, predict how an unfamiliar interaction fails in one of the six states, then reproduce it. Use Tan's verification practice to inspect the running interface. Explain which model and context choice you retained, its observed failures, and how the fallback helps the user finish the task.

Discovery, shaping, strategy & measurement

Take a candidate domain into discovery. Learn its language with users, decide what deserves to be built, and express the important rules in a working model.
126 hrsWeeks 17-30
Learn
  • Outcomes, continuous customer contact, opportunity solution trees
  • Ubiquitous language, core and supporting domains, explicit rules, counterexamples, and the link between domain models and code
  • Identifying and testing assumptions; interviewing without leading the participant
  • Stocks, flows, reinforcing and balancing loops, delays, boundaries, leverage points

Ask about past behavior

Produce

05

AI-native & agentic product engineering

Practice the full lifecycle of an AI product. Judge success by the outcome; "More autonomous" alone does not meet that standard.
144 hrsWeeks 49-64
Approaches to compare
  • Existing manual process or deterministic software
  • Single model call or retrieval-assisted response
  • Fixed workflow, bounded agent, or cooperating agents
  • Choose feasible candidates for the task. Compare outcome quality, total cost, latency, and human intervention; implement only the comparisons that answer a real uncertainty.
Direction and supervision
  • Investigation versus implementation authority; tool permissions, untrusted inputs, and human approval
  • Durable role records, deterministic monitors, ownership, handoffs, and backpressure
  • Model selection by observed quality, review effort, elapsed time, cost, and available quota
Data, context, and evaluation
  • Representative cases, error types, held-out evaluation, repeated trials, and human calibration of model graders
  • Retrieval quality versus answer quality; missing, stale, conflicting, or unauthorized context
  • Human reliance, correction, fallback, approval behavior, displaced work, and compounding failure
Produce
Give each tool an understandable contract: validated inputs, permissions, side effects, retry behavior, recovery, and machine-readable errors. A tool that runs code or searches broadly can be appropriate when you control its environment and authority and test its results. Prevent duplicate effects where possible. Otherwise, reconcile state or require human intervention before retrying. The tool limit bounds this exercise. Evaluate the whole workflow, including its persisted results.
Use a project-specific verification skill to exercise the running app. Follow the affected paths in the feature map, check failure and persistence behavior, and capture evidence for the reviewer. Tan's verification-skill example shows the structure using a fictional app and CLI. Run Chen's supervision and routing exercises, Emanuel's fresh-session and small-tool exercises, and Yegge's monitor and recovery exercises with your existing tools. Complete Tan's recurring-defect and maintenance exercise on the same workflow. Establish reliable checks before the two-worker drill. Compare it with the serial approach and record whether the extra coordination earns its cost.
Pass condition

Build and evaluate the bounded agent, then justify deploying, limiting, or rejecting it against the baseline. Before comparing results, set acceptance criteria for outcome quality, cost, latency, reliability, risk, and human workload. A supported decision to withhold deployment can pass.

Independently identify a case where the agent follows the specification but violates the domain rule. Explain the permission boundary, reject a hostile instruction in tool output, and recover from interrupted work. The monitor must make no model calls while idle. Check where a model grader disagrees with human judgment and explain how you handle any disagreement. Keep acceptance cases separate from development examples and repeat selected high-risk cases. Report variation and errors by user or task group alongside aggregate scores, and explain the limits of the sample.

Test what can be removed

After a model, context, or tool change, remove one planning step, memory mechanism, wrapper, or extra agent in a controlled comparison. Hold the task set and acceptance criteria fixed. Compare quality, elapsed time, human intervention, and total cost including review. Keep or restore the mechanism based on the result. Preserve required permissions and outcome checks during the experiment.

Use the same evaluation work to separate retrieval failure from answer failure. Assess whether a prompt change, retrieval, another model, or fine-tuning would address the failure. Explain your choice; training a model is optional. Test regression detection and the user's correction or fallback path.

06

Integrated capstone

Finish and operate the product developed through the earlier phases. Address a recurring problem with reachable users. A generic task manager, habit tracker, or chat wrapper does not qualify.
72 hrsWeeks 65-72
Requirements for the workflow
  • People already use an awkward workaround
  • The task recurs and success is observable
  • You can reach real users
  • A bounded AI capability has a testable opportunity to improve the task
Case study arc
  • Problem → evidence → system model → rejected ideas → chosen behavior → design → architecture → agent boundary → evaluation → measured result → remaining uncertainty
Deliver the product and its evidence
Include failures and reversals so readers can assess your judgment. Show the user's correction and fallback path, and the comparison that justified keeping or removing a step or component from the agent workflow. A prototype that failed its deployment criteria belongs in the evidence with the reason you withheld it. Label model claims as observed, measured, inferred, or speculative. Hiding mistakes makes the case study promotional.
Pass condition

A senior engineer, designer, and PM can each inspect your evidence independently. They can understand why the problem persisted, why the intervention had leverage, why the design and architecture fit, and how you controlled the agent. They can also judge whether the product improved the system or shifted cost or failure elsewhere.

Defend the last domain-driven model change without a generated explanation. Have the reviewer choose an unfamiliar rule change or failure, then show that you can reason through it and verify the result.

09Make the work visible

Turn skill into credible evidence

When the general market contracts, referrals and visible proof carry more weight than application volume. Give people work they can inspect, build relationships with practitioners, and choose a domain. Prepare for a job search while you are still employed.

Habit 01 · Publish

Make the work findable

Each month, remove sensitive details from one weekly artifact and publish it: a teardown, an ADR, or an eval write-up. Use the capstone case study as your resume.

Habit 02 · People

Have two conversations a month

Talk to people already doing product-engineer, FDE, or applied-AI work. Ask what their week looks like and what they screen for. Keep up two substantive conversations a month throughout the course, prioritizing them over 500 untargeted applications.

Habit 03 · Domain

Commit to a domain by week 30

Choose a candidate domain and reachable users by week 16, then use Phase 3 to validate the problem and commit by week 30. If discovery exposes a poor fit, make one domain change by week 22 and repeat the essential interviews. Carry over the evaluation method, then replace cases that no longer represent the task. Favor constraints that require specific knowledge, such as money, health, logistics, or compliance. Use that domain for the architecture, agent, and capstone work.

Career milestones
While employed, work toward saving six months of expenses and complete one real interview loop every quarter or two. Use that market evidence to prepare before you need a job and to keep fear from driving the plan.
10Use the practice time well

Five ways to spend time without learning enough

Trap 01

Switching languages

Use the stack you ship and instrument fastest for the first 90 days.

Trap 02

Prompt collections

A prompt collection is unreliable without datasets, graders, and failure analysis to test it.

Trap 03

Multi-agent novelty

Compare feasible designs on the same task and constraints. Keep extra agents only when their measured contribution justifies coordination and review.

Trap 04

Dribbble imitation

Copying an interface without understanding its flows or users demonstrates imitation rather than design judgment.

Trap 05

Pattern collecting

Learn a pattern's constraints, consequences, and evidence so you can explain when to use it. Knowing its name alone is not enough.
