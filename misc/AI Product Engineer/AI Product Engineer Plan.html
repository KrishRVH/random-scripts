The role this plan develops

Own the decisions around the code

Learn to discover, build, and operate useful products while directing AI-assisted development. Own the problem definition, design, acceptance criteria, permissions, and operating decisions. Use SVPG's four risks to assess the result with users and domain experts. Study model-dependent features as an additional capability, and ship them when evidence supports their value to users.

Risk 01 · Value

Will anyone choose this?

A real user chooses it over the workaround they have today.

Risk 02 · Usability

Can they figure it out?

They finish the task without you there to explain it.

Risk 03 · Feasibility

Can the team build and operate it?

The team can ship and operate it under real constraints, including failure.

Risk 04 · Viability

Does it work for the business?

It fits the strategy, costs, legal duties, and organizational limits.

At completion you can
  • 01Discover and validate a meaningful user problem.
  • 02Design a polished, accessible end-to-end experience.
  • 03Model the domain with users, and carry its language and rules into code and tests.
  • 04Architect and operate an evolvable production system.
  • 05Build agentic capability with tools, evals, security, and observability.
  • 06Measure outcomes: show how the shipped code changed behavior.
Develop judgment in work that is harder to commoditize: deciding, integrating, evaluating, and taking responsibility for outcomes.

Direct and review the agent's work

Before consulting an agent's answer, spend up to 20 minutes making a prediction about the decision you most need to practice. Check your reasoning against code and primary sources. Use this exercise to develop judgment, then divide investigation, planning, and implementation according to the needs of the task.

Give the agent the intended outcome, constraints, allowed actions, and required evidence. Inspect changed boundaries and behavior with serious consequences directly. Use automated checks and targeted inspection for routine changes. Require the result as an evidence report and read the code when its risk tier is not low, following Chen's review tiers. Record what you checked, what you delegated, and what remains uncertain. Follow Tan's verification practice to inspect the running result. The block is complete only when the evidence supports acceptance.

Use real work throughout the 72 weeks. Keep the decision log as a record of what your reviews caught and how your specifications and verification methods developed. Before widening delegated work, complete the engineering checks in Phases 1-2. If a check exposes a gap, narrow the next task, revisit the assigned topic, and practice on a changed case.

Four practitioner case studies

Learn to direct work you can trust

Lauren Tan, Kun Chen, Jeffrey Emanuel, and Steve Yegge use different mechanisms to address recurring problems in agent work. Study how they define rules, assign authority, focus attention, plan before costly implementation, verify behavior, and recover interrupted work. Apply each exercise to your product in the phase that teaches its prerequisites. The comparison shows where their practices agree and where you need to choose.

Lauren Tan / poteto · Trust, verification, and an agent-friendly codebase+

Set up the environment so agents do the right thing by default

In her 2026 Cursor Compile talk, Tan reports shipping 2,000 pull requests to production in one month while working on GrokBot at SpaceX AI. Her thesis is that throughput follows trust. Set up the environment for your agents well enough and one person, or one team, produces high-quality code at far higher rates. She prefers the Michelin kitchen to the software factory as the analogy. The head chef no longer cooks each component but still owns the final dish and designs the stations, training, equipment, and staffing that make it good.

The practice started when she was asked to fix performance in the new Cursor agent window. She spent her early weeks reading Chrome DevTools traces and heap snapshots by hand while pull requests landed faster than she could check them. The way out was a verification skill that taught the agent to run the application, take traces over the Chrome DevTools Protocol, and find hot spots itself. Every later skill, tool, and codebase change laddered up to the same idea: she was the bottleneck, so she had to move her engineering knowledge into the environment her agents work in. During the weekly build block, use these techniques to give the agent a clear outcome and room to explore, and require evidence from the running product before accepting its work.

The trust curve

Tan plots trust against the number of agents you can run. At one to five agents you babysit every chat, course-correct constantly, and nothing happens when you step away. She calls this the hardest phase to leave, because the way out is not obvious. The curve rises through five to ten, ten to twenty, hundreds, and thousands, and flattens toward the top. Spawning a hundred sub-agents or cloud agents before you trust them produces sloppy pull requests, regressions, and shipped bugs.

Verification for correctnessTeach the agent to run the app and collect empirical evidence that the feature does what you want. Verification skills sit at the low end of a spectrum whose far end is formal methods in Lean or TLA+. Few teams can run formal methods, and verification skills alone get you very far.
Skills for qualityVerification proves correctness and yields real performance telemetry, but says nothing about code quality. Playbooks such as pstack teach agents to debug, build features, and prototype the way an experienced engineer would. Experienced engineers contribute here by building a team repository of skills.
Agent-friendly architectureRefactor or rewrite so the codebase does the right thing by default. Tan calls this one of the most important investments a team can make if agents will write most future code. Greenfield and brownfield codebases start this from different places.

Make verification repeatable

  • A feature map gives context and a CLI gives control. Put a CLI inside the skill directory so every session drives the real app the same way instead of writing throwaway scripts that differ between runs. Invest in it until it handles the cases your product has.
  • Use the existing test runner, API, or browser driver to launch the app, seed data, use test identities, drive interactions, and collect screenshots and runtime traces. Provide subcommands that work together, useful --help, machine-readable output, errors that explain what to do next, and --dry-run for destructive operations.
  • The feature map was born from vague bug reports: a cropped screenshot in Slack with three question marks that a capable agent could not locate in the product. Modeled on a sitemap, it records what each feature does, how a user reaches it by shortcut or element, and how the agent can exercise it. It lives in the skill directory as materialized memory, and an automation keeps it current with the app.
  • The combination let agents reproduce reports from internal and external users on their own. Tan treats the skill as critical infrastructure, maintains it constantly, and recommends a daily maintenance pass. In pstack, use /create-verification-skill to build the project-specific skill and /maintain-verification-skill to maintain it. Establish reliable verification before scaling parallel work.

Resolve uncertainty before implementation

  • Ask the agent to restate the underlying problem before proposing your own solution. Correct misunderstandings while leaving implementation choices open.
  • Use /how to trace behavior, /why to investigate rationale, /recall to recover relevant work, and /teach to explain the reasoning and tradeoffs in terms you can assess.
  • For a shared package, start with a caller's usage example or tutorial, then sketch types and public signatures. Keep tutorials, how-to guides, reference, and explanation distinct with /technical-writing.
  • Prototype competing approaches under the same constraints and compare their behavior. For larger architectural boundaries, use /architect: establish the problem, compare design sketches with an independent model, and implement the strongest design. Rework it if runtime evidence or repeated caller workarounds expose a flaw.

Whenever you correct your agent

Tan calls this the one slide to take away. Each time you intervene, ask which of five layers would stop the mistake from recurring, and prefer the earliest layer you can afford. The first two enforce. The next two guide, and an agent can skip a rule or a busy pilot can ignore it. The last works only when a human reads every line, which fails at the rate agents open pull requests.

1 · CodebaseChange the architecture or data structures so the mistake becomes categorically impossible. This is the strongest fix because agents extend whatever they see.
2 · Static analysisLint rules, compiler diagnostics, and continuous integration turn a correction into a constraint that holds without you present.
3 · Rules and BugbotWritten guidance and automated review catch what static analysis cannot express, but the agent may not read them and the reviewer may not run them.
4 · SkillsPlaybooks that teach a workflow. They work when the agent loads them and do nothing when it does not.
5 · Style guideEnforced only by humans in code review. Use review comments to discover what the other four layers are missing, then move the lesson into an earlier layer.
Your codebase is memory

Agents extend the patterns in their context window, and the files they open are that context. They do not refactor on every pull request. So one innocent workaround, or a comment explaining it, spreads like a virus: agents copy it, and each copy makes the next copy likelier. Within days or weeks the workaround is the pattern and the product is slow and hard to maintain. In the Cursor codebase, Tan noticed agents using code comments as justification for papering over a problem with a short-term fix instead of solving it. Her team's framework bans code comments for that reason.

The right target is a codebase you would be happy for an agent to copy: so conventional and locked down that it is annoying for humans and ideal for an agent with minimal context. That matters because the next contributor may be a designer, product manager, or executive piloting an agent with very little context. Every team needs a gardener who notices unwanted growth early and removes it before it propagates.

Delete tech debtRemove existing anti-patterns, because each one is a template the next agent will reuse.
Keep one paved pathOne conventional way to do each thing, with enough guidance in code, lint, and CI that agents do not have to guess.
Lint against anti-patternsWhen you spot tech debt, your first instinct should be a lint rule. A rule stops the bleeding before you have time to clean up. Then schedule agents to do the cleanup.
A framework designed for agents

GrokBot runs on Dune, a client framework Tan's team built from lessons learned fixing Cursor's agent window. Its principle is that agents love shortcuts, so make the easy path the right path. Five nouns organize every app, each with one place in the tree and one job at runtime: a feature owns one folder of product UI, an entrypoint is one view a user can open, a transcript card is the feature-owned body for one entry type, the client holds durable renderer state behind hooks and commands, with a single writer, and the host provides always-on behavior behind a typed contract.

Process boundaries are visible in the tree. A folder tells an agent where code runs and which imports are legal: renderer code, serving processes such as host extensions and the Electron main process, and a shared folder for cross-process types, joined by typed edges. Dune enforces the boundary through the import and dependency graph. The motivating defect was slow main-process code accidentally imported into the renderer, where a task longer than 16 milliseconds drops below 60 frames per second, and longer than 8 milliseconds drops below 120. The framework is the point, not Dune itself: encode the tribal knowledge that once lived in style guides and review comments into the codebase, so an agent with little context or reasoning still writes good code.

The outer loop

Once the environment earns trust, the same pieces compound outward. GrokBot connects to Slack, Datadog, Sentry, PlanetScale, and whatever else the team uses, aggregates that information, and kicks off cloud agents. Routines subscribe to Slack threads or Sentry alerts and act automatically. Cursor automations and the Agent SDK reuse the same skills and verification to run additional bots for more complex tasks. Tan distinguishes individual automation, the line cook agent, from team automation, the factory agent. Neither needs a sophisticated company brain, she argues, because agents are already good at using tools.

Her example is an on-call assistant. A user posted a screen recording of a sidebar that kept reopening. The automation reproduced the bug on the prior commit, confirmed it was already fixed on main, and replied in the thread with the evidence and a link to the cloud agent run. Other automations open pull requests. The value comes from the lower layers compounding: codebase, lint, rules, skills, and verification.

Practice in Phases 2-5. Test these on the same product:
  • Write an invalid domain example. Enforce its rule in a type, constructor, API, or runtime check, then ask a fresh agent to extend the design.
  • Show a defect the verifier catches and one it cannot. Check authorization, persisted state, and concurrent effects directly when they matter.
  • Compare two design sketches, divide verification coverage, and commission a fresh review. Explain what each exercise establishes and why model agreement is not proof.
  • Trace a recurring defect to its cause. Add a narrow check, test it against a legitimate case, and remove a stale instruction or unnecessary wrapper.
  • Place yourself on the trust curve. Name the number of agents you run without babysitting and the failure that keeps you there. Pick the one investment from the three trust moves that would move you one band to the right, and measure it four weeks later.
  • Keep a correction log for two weeks. For each intervention, choose the earliest layer that would have prevented it. Ship at least one codebase change and one lint rule from the log, and record whether the correction recurred.
  • Run a gardener pass. Find one workaround or explanatory comment that has been copied more than once. Write the lint rule first, confirm it fails on the copies and passes on a legitimate case, then have an agent clean up the instances.
  • Make one boundary visible in the tree. Choose a process, layer, or ownership boundary that lives only in convention, express it in folders, and enforce it with an import rule. Ask a fresh agent to add a feature and check whether the rule guided it without instructions.
  • Build one outer-loop routine. Connect one event source, such as an error alert or a bug report channel, to an agent that reproduces the report with the verification skill and replies with evidence. Do not let it open a pull request until its reports have been right for a month.

Kun Chen · Authority, attention, and supervision+

Spend attention on decisions, not mechanics

In his 2026 talk on high-throughput agentic engineering, Chen runs 36 projects and seven or more parallel workers from one session. He talks only to a liaison, the first mate, which dispatches workers into isolated worktrees, supervises them, escalates only decisions, and reports outcomes. His binding constraint is attention, not tokens. The rules keep his attention on what to build and accept. Firstmate expresses them through plain-text instructions, scripts, and state conventions that can be used by any harness.

Make authority explicit

  • Keep one point of contact that holds your intent across every project. It never does project work itself, so it stays available to you. It reports outcomes, consequences, and decisions while workers handle implementation details.
  • Classify each task by its authority. A scout task investigates and leaves a report without touching the project. A ship task delivers an authorized change. Keep the delivery path, whether a full gate, a direct PR, or a local merge, separate from merge authority, and write both down per repository.
  • Set review requirements by the consequences of failure. If you would have asked a colleague to review the change, run the full pipeline of review, tests, docs, and lint in an isolated worktree before the push reaches the remote. Grant self-merge only where nothing depends on the result yet, or where a merge does not release immediately and you can test before release.
  • Authority is explicit, never inferred. Standing permissions never quietly widen, evidence never authorizes an action by itself, and a red check is never merged without a named waiver. A current, concrete instruction overrides a standing rule, but never by analogy and never for another object.
  • Write dispatch rules that map each type of task to eligible harnesses, models, and effort levels, with a reason for each choice. Use remaining quota to choose among eligible models; quota alone must not determine eligibility. Test a new model before assuming it performs better.

Review evidence, not every line

  • Require each change as an evidence report: the intent you gave, what changed, a risk tier, the live scenarios run against the intent with skipped ones named, and logs. Accept a low-risk, well-tested change on its evidence. Read the code when the tier is not low.
  • Save decisions in durable records that survive a restart. Append status updates to a log and keep open decisions visible until you close them. Ask for a digest of completed work, work in progress, and decisions waiting on you. Queue a follow-up behind current work instead of interrupting it.
  • Use a deterministic watcher to monitor worker state and wake the model when a decision is needed. Read working, blocked, and idle states according to the harness's definitions. Record unknown state explicitly; a quiet pane is not proof of a stuck worker.
  • Present decisions with evidence. Prototype two or three variants in parallel, review them on one interactive page with screenshots, tradeoffs, and a recommendation, and record your choices as form state rather than prose.
  • Set a compaction threshold once and stop managing context by hand. Hide tool output while work runs. Let the liaison handle routine retries, and have it escalate only review-ready work, findings, blockers, credentials, and destructive actions.

Jeffrey Emanuel · Plans that survive the conversation+

Resolve mistakes before they become expensive

Emanuel's Flywheel separates planning, work packets, and code by the cost of correcting a mistake. The plan fits in one context, and self-contained packets form a dependency graph. He spends most of the effort on the plan and packets, where a correction takes a reasoning edit rather than code changes and cleanup. Workers pull ready packets into fresh sessions without preceding chat. Apply these practices at the scale your work needs.

Plan before packets

  • Write the plan as one document that fits in context: user, constraints, invariants, unknowns, workflows, and tradeoffs. Ask two or more independent models for competing plans, then merge them as explicit diffs you accept or reject with a reason.
  • Keep revising the plan while the product design is unsettled. Create packets once the workflow questions are resolved and the remaining work needs to be sequenced.
  • Convert the plan directly into real packets in the tracker, never into pseudo-tasks in prose. Each packet carries the outcome, acceptance criteria and failure modes, tests, reasoning, and dependencies, so a worker never needs the plan.
  • Polish the packet set in rounds until responses shrink and stop changing. Then ask whether completing every open packet would close the gap to the goal. If not, revise the packets before resuming.
The Complete Flywheel Guide

Run from the graph, not the chat

  • Store tasks and dependencies as durable records beside the code, and pull only ready work: open, unblocked, and not deferred. Use the graph to find what everything depends on, the bottlenecks, and the critical path before trusting intuition about priority.
  • Give each packet to a fresh worker with no preceding chat, and have every worker reread the operating manual after compaction. If heavy thinking happens during implementation, the plan or the packets were too thin.
  • Turn a repeated inspection into a rerunnable command only when its total cost, including upkeep, beats the manual check. Small commits, advisory file reservations, and a mechanical guard on destructive commands prevent collisions without locks.
  • Review in two passes: self-review after each packet until no defects remain, then a fresh-eyes review from another session. Keep a separate polish pass for friction and error messages once correctness is settled.

Steve Yegge · Continuity and recovery+

Design roles that outlive the session

Yegge runs persistent operational roles, each with a standing responsibility, over a durable task graph. His rule is that crons watch and models act: cheap deterministic monitors detect conditions and wake a model only when judgment is needed. Sessions are temporary, so work moves through handoffs, leases, and gates. Apply these mechanisms at the scale your work needs.

Give each role a charter

  • Define a role by its purpose, permissions, durable state, human owner, and escalation path, and let it own routine decisions in its domain. Separate the role, which persists, from the session, which ends.
  • Use a deterministic monitor to watch system state and invoke a model only for an ambiguous event. Idle monitoring makes no model calls.
  • End each session with a handoff. The agent finishes its current step and records completed work, pending work, and the next safe action. Write the handoff while the relevant context is available, before reaching the compaction limit.
  • Use the audit trail as the record of completed work and keep closed entries immutable. Use postmortems to improve the system, and give every role a path to escalate to a human.
The Shape of Things to Come

Make progress idempotent

  • Move work through explicit claims, leases, and gates so nothing is orphaned. A worker that dies releases its lease, and a sweeper finds stuck or dropped work and nudges it.
  • Aim for nondeterministic idempotence. Any session may take an unpredictable path, but the defined workflow completes as long as you keep attaching sessions to the persistent role and its state.
  • A timeout means the observer stopped waiting; the worker may still be running. Check the durable state before restarting anything, and never repeat an effect or overwrite another worker's work.
  • Apply backpressure. When verification cannot keep up with delivery, limit incoming work or change the validation strategy. Yegge's merge queue recovered only when he stopped reviewing every failure himself and gave a role clear heuristics.

Routine work to scriptsTan adds a control CLI and narrow checks. Chen keeps logic in scripts that fail safely and wakes the model only for decisions. Emanuel prices a rerunnable inspection against its upkeep. Yegge's crons watch and models act. Practice: Phase 5 monitor with no idle model calls.
Recover without repeatingTan checks concurrent effects directly. Chen checks what a worker completed before restarting it and never discards unlanded work. Emanuel makes packets restartable and forbids disturbing another worker's work. Yegge records completed work, pending effects, and the next safe action. Practice: Phase 4 interrupted operation, Phase 5 recovery.
Agreement is not proofTan prototypes competing approaches and treats model agreement as no proof. Chen reviews parallel variants and tests a new model before trusting it. Emanuel merges competing plans as explicit diffs and reviews with fresh eyes. Yegge diagnoses failures in parallel rather than trusting one bisection. Practice: Phase 3 competing plan, Phase 5 removal experiment.
Earn its costTan removes stale instructions and unnecessary wrappers, and writes a lint rule before cleaning up a spreading pattern. Chen runs the full gate only where a colleague would have reviewed and adds a supervisor only when the first cannot answer him. Emanuel stops refining when changes shrink and asks whether the open packets close the gap. Yegge limits incoming work when verification cannot keep up. Practice: Phase 5 removal experiment, Trap 03.
Where they differ, so test it on your product
IsolationEmanuel runs every worker on one branch with advisory file reservations and small commits. Chen gives each task a disposable worktree. Yegge gives each worker its own clone and a merge queue. Measure collisions, reconciliation time, and review load on your product.
RolesYegge specializes persistent roles. Emanuel keeps workers fungible so any worker can resume any packet. Chen keeps one liaison and adds supervisors only by scope. Decide which failure you can afford: a single point of failure or diluted context.
Human reviewYegge expects per-change human approval to give way to rounds of agentic review. Chen tiers human review by risk behind a gate. Tan requires evidence from the running product for every accepted change and treats a style guide enforced only in review as a hole at agent pull-request rates. Set your own tier by consequence and keep the Phase 4-5 pass conditions.
Where effort goesEmanuel puts most effort into the plan and packets before code. Tan resolves uncertainty with runnable prototypes and moves each correction into the codebase or static analysis so it stops recurring. Chen puts it into briefs, policies, and evidence review. Record where your defects originated and shift effort there.
Use your existing tools or a local simulation to test these practices. Keep a practice when its results justify the complexity, supervision, and cost. Use one product for all four case studies and their exercises.

Six interface states
  • Normal: content is available and the user can perform the task.
  • Empty: no records or results are available. Explain why and offer a useful next step.
  • Loading: an operation is in progress. Show that it is underway and preserve context.
  • Error: an operation failed. Explain what happened and how to recover.

  • Disabled: an action is unavailable because a prerequisite or permission is missing. Explain what is required.

Approaches to compare
  • Existing manual process or deterministic software
  • Single model call or retrieval-assisted response
  • Fixed workflow, bounded agent, or cooperating agents

Make the work visible

Turn skill into credible evidence

When the general market contracts, referrals and visible proof carry more weight than application volume. Give people work they can inspect, build relationships with practitioners, and choose a domain. Prepare for a job search while you are still employed.

Habit 01 · Publish

Make the work findable

Each month, remove sensitive details from one weekly artifact and publish it: a teardown, an ADR, or an eval write-up. Use the capstone case study as your resume.

Habit 02 · People

Have two conversations a month

Talk to people already doing product-engineer, FDE, or applied-AI work. Ask what their week looks like and what they screen for. Keep up two substantive conversations a month throughout the course, prioritizing them over 500 untargeted applications.

Habit 03 · Domain

Commit to a domain by week 30

Choose a candidate domain and reachable users by week 16, then use Phase 3 to validate the problem and commit by week 30. If discovery exposes a poor fit, make one domain change by week 22 and repeat the essential interviews. Carry over the evaluation method, then replace cases that no longer represent the task. Favor constraints that require specific knowledge, such as money, health, logistics, or compliance. Use that domain for the architecture, agent, and capstone work.

Career milestones
While employed, work toward saving six months of expenses and complete one real interview loop every quarter or two. Use that market evidence to prepare before you need a job and to keep fear from driving the plan.
Use the practice time well

Five ways to spend time without learning enough

Trap 01

Switching languages

Use the stack you ship and instrument fastest for the first 90 days.

Trap 02

Prompt collections

Test prompts with datasets, graders, and failure analysis before relying on them.

Trap 03

Multi-agent novelty

Compare feasible designs on the same task and constraints. Keep extra agents only when their measured contribution justifies coordination and review. Add a second supervisor only after the first is too busy to answer you, and split it by scope, not by count.

Trap 04

Dribbble imitation

Copying an interface without understanding its flows or users demonstrates imitation rather than design judgment.

Trap 05

Pattern collecting

Explain when a pattern applies by studying its constraints, consequences, and supporting evidence.
