01 / The learning contract

Use a real product to develop engineering judgment.

Practice understanding, designing, diagnosing, and evolving software in a real product. Assess your progress by what you can explain and do yourself, including your ability to judge substantial work you delegate.

What you develop

Build durable engineering judgment through structured study, hands-on reasoning, and independent assessment. Use selected primary texts for each module; consult supporting books and references when an exercise exposes a specific gap.

What AI contributes

Apply the four practitioners' methods throughout the year: executable verification, architectural constraints, bounded delegation, durable work state, task decomposition, model routing, handoffs, event-driven operations, and learning from operational results. Practice these methods within each engineering module.

Scope: AI-augmented product and software engineering is the main track. One module also develops applied-AI product skills: tools, context, retrieval, evaluation, and safe actions. Training foundation models, collecting agent frameworks, and building a large personal orchestration platform are outside the core.

Time commitment. 52 weeks × 9 hours = 468 hours. Reading, recruitment, setup, failed experiments, reviews, and portfolio work all fit inside that budget. This is a selective senior-engineer curriculum, not a promise to master every field or guarantee employment.
Start with your existing tools

The first session: 90 minutes.

0–20 min

Choose a product or workflow.

Pick an existing product or a small real workflow you can observe. State who uses it, what they are trying to accomplish, and one frustrating failure. An internal request-review tool or a maintainer's recurring workflow is enough. Access to the people and problem matters more than novelty.

20–65 min

Test your current model.

Trace one path without AI. Explain the state, effects, and likely failure, then run it and compare the result with your prediction. Read a short relevant source section and write what you misunderstood. Use this diagnostic to find your starting point.

65–90 min

Write the next bounded task.

Define one outcome, one prohibited action, and the evidence needed. Schedule the next study block and save a note about the current state. Continue with week 1 using your existing setup; leave installation of the four practitioners' stacks for exercises that need them.

These 90 minutes replace the first protected-practice block. Use your familiar language, database, editor, and one coding agent. Reuse your current CI and standards. Start on any date; the numbered weeks are relative. Access to a second model helps selected critiques, but no particular subscription or hardware purchase is required.

Adapt to prior mastery: you may already pass parts of a module. Demonstrate the unfamiliar transfer case first, then use the time you free up for a harder real failure or a weak adjacent skill. Familiarity with a topic name does not exempt you from its AI-workflow application or assessment.

02 / How learning happens

Practice independently, then apply the skill with AI.

Study→Predict→Attempt→Use AI→Verify→Transfer
Learning mode

Do the reasoning you intend to assess.

Before asking for a solution, write a hypothesis, sketch the boundary, predict a failure, or implement the small mechanism. Use the debugger, compiler, source, and documentation yourself. AI may ask questions, explain a concept, or supply one hint; do not let it perform the exact skill you intend to assess.

Try independently for 15–25 minutes as a useful default, then seek a hint if needed. Close the answer and reconstruct the reasoning after the hint. Focus this time on reasoning rather than hand-writing boilerplate.

Delivery mode

Delegate what you can supervise.

Use agents for implementation breadth, repetitive edits, test scaffolding, alternatives, and independent critique. Retain ownership of scope, invariants, permissions, acceptance criteria, and the final decision. Inspect the important code and run the real path.

Switch back to learning mode when you cannot explain a proposed abstraction, a failure, or an important test. “The reviewer approved it” is not a substitute for understanding.

An Anthropic study of 52 developers learning an unfamiliar library found weaker immediate mastery with AI assistance. Its qualitative analysis associated more concept-focused interaction with better learning. That supports taking the learning risk seriously, but it does not establish long-term effects for senior engineers or validate this curriculum's exact schedule. [L1]

1. Understanding

Explain the mechanism, invariant, tradeoff, and a counterexample without AI or prepared prose. Reading fluent explanations does not count.

2. Application

Use the concept in a real change. Show code or user behavior plus independent evidence. Identify what your evidence cannot establish.

3. Transfer

After 7–14 days, solve a related problem with a different constraint or unfamiliar code. Notes and API docs are allowed after your initial explanation; record any hints.

What “high return” means here

Choose activities that teach a reusable concept, expose a meaningful failure, improve a real workflow, and leave a small reusable result. Reject exercises whose main achievement is installing a tool, reproducing a famous setup, or counting generated output. A simulator you later discard can teach more than a functioning fleet of agents.

One principle card per week is enough: state the rule in your own words, name the conditions where it applies, give a counterexample, link the exercise, and record the next recall date. A justified rejection or deletion is as valuable as a new rule. Treat the 52 cards as judgments to revisit.

Level What you can do Next action

For each module, assess explanation, diagnosis, and design judgment separately. Aim for independent performance in all three and one demonstrated transfer. These are working learning criteria, not a validated certification scale. Record a short link or reference to the evidence for each assessment.

If a core gap remains, use the next application block to work on it and drop an optional polish task to stay within the week. Revisit one older topic during the weekly recall block. After a failed test, work on the exposed weakness and continue from there.

Time for the four practitioners: spend 39 protected hours studying their mechanisms and apply them in the build, verification, and practice blocks throughout the year. The total already includes those 39 hours. All sixteen lesson IDs below recur in the weekly track.

Library rule: protect time for the active module’s primary source and the Pragmatic companion. Use supporting books and references when a concrete exercise exposes the matching gap. See the reading library.

Busy-week floor: 4½ hours. Preserve 60 minutes of study, 30 of practitioner work, 60 of independent practice, 90 of application, and 30 of recall/verification. Halve the scope and extend the calendar; a half-week does not complete a nine-hour week. After a missed week, resume the last incomplete learning objective at the usual pace.

The year-long companion / 26 protected hours

Keep The Pragmatic Programmer active for all 52 weeks.

Reserve 30 minutes of the weekly foundation block for a selected theme or deliberate reapplication. Use the build and recall blocks to test whether the idea improves your work.

01 · Read

Find one relevant claim.

Use the current module to choose a short section. Stop when you can state the mechanism rather than when a chapter ends.

02 · Reconstruct

Close the book.

Explain how the idea works, name a counterexample, and identify the condition under which the advice stops helping.

03 · Apply

Apply the idea in your work.

Use it in code, debugging, architecture, product work, testing, communication, or delegation during the scheduled practice.

04 · Judge

Keep, adapt, or reject.

Record the result and cost. Adopt the advice as policy only after it proves useful in your work.

04 / The four practitioner field guides

Sixteen practices to test during the year.

Each introduction describes an approach drawn from the practitioner's sources. The lessons, exercises, and mastery tests adapt those approaches for this curriculum. Use the lesson IDs to find their applications in the weekly track.

Poteto

Encode constraints and verify application behavior.

Pstack emphasizes architectural constraints, task-specific procedures, and verification against the actual application. Its verification generator starts with the repository's existing ways to run, drive, observe, and isolate the application. Its architect skill uses structural sketches before implementation. [P1] [P2] [P3]

P1Constraints before reminders

Foundation: Information hiding, domain modeling, and type safety.

Practice: Pick a repeated misuse and write the invalid example first. Redesign its constructor, API, or dependency boundary so the misuse is rejected or localized. Ask an agent with no prior task context to extend the design.

Mastery: Explain which invariant is enforced and which still requires a runtime check. Assess complexity separately from line count.

P2Build an observable feedback loop

Foundation: Testability, debugging interfaces, and behavioral oracles.

Practice: Start with the existing test runner or HTTP/browser harness. Add only a missing control or observation. Drive a success and a failure path from a clean state; inspect the relevant persisted result.

Mastery: Show one defect the harness detects and one it cannot. Screenshots do not prove authorization, durability, or absence of races.

P3Use the right kind of parallel reasoning

Foundation: Design alternatives, experiment design, and skeptical review.

Practice: Compare two alternative sketches for one consequential boundary. Use parallel workers for separate verification tasks and a fresh reviewer for a finished diff. Hold the requirement constant and compare the findings.

Mastery: Explain why alternatives, coverage, and critique are different activities. Two agreeing models are not two independent proofs.

P4Fix recurring design problems and retire stale rules

Foundation: Refactoring, architectural fitness functions, and maintenance economics.

Practice: Sample recent changes. Fix one recurring smell at its cause. Encode a narrow check if it prevents recurrence, then challenge the check with a legitimate case. Delete a stale instruction or unnecessary wrapper.

Mastery: Defend the rule's cost in false positives and state when you will revisit it. Keep design rationale that code cannot express.

In this curriculum, keep human judgment and learning explicit. Adapt pstack instructions to the exercise; cloud infrastructure is optional. Reported PR volume does not establish a productivity guarantee.

Kun Chen

Give delegated work a contract, boundary, and owner.

Firstmate separates the human-facing liaison from workers and persistent supervisors. Its architecture uses event-driven supervision and durable task state. No-mistakes validates in isolation and distinguishes mechanical repairs from decisions that alter intent; quota-axi supplies quota observations. [C1] [C2] [C3] [C4]

C1Separate investigation from authorization to ship

Foundation: Requirements analysis, delegation, and decision rights.

Practice: Write a read-only investigation brief and an implementation brief for the same issue. Include unknowns, allowed files and tools, the stopping rule, expected output, and who may publish.

Mastery: Catch a technically competent answer that solves the wrong problem. Escalate high-impact uncertainty even when an action is reversible.

C2Isolate changes and bind approval to evidence

Foundation: Git, reproducible builds, capability boundaries, and integration.

Practice: Use a separate branch or worktree, or an isolated environment. Record the reviewed revision, checks, and artifact. Change the revision after approval and demonstrate why the old verdict is insufficient.

Mastery: Explain the protection isolation provides and its limits. Shared databases, credentials, deployment targets, and semantic conflicts remain possible.

C3Choose a model that meets the task requirements

Foundation: Cost modeling, workload classification, and calibration.

Practice: On two ordinary tasks, compare a fast eligible model with a stronger one. Record outcome, your review time, elapsed time, and spend. Add a simple routing rule only if the difference is useful.

Mastery: Distinguish capability requirements from quota convenience. A depleted quota must not silently lower a security or reasoning requirement.

C4Limit the supervisor's context and authority

Foundation: Control-plane design, observability, and failure detection.

Practice: Let one liaison summarize two isolated tasks, then interrupt a worker. Require current state, evidence, and an explicit owner before any restart. Local processes are sufficient for the exercise.

Mastery: Explain why an unreachable worker is not necessarily dead. Show when direct human supervision is simpler than another manager.

Start with a single agent and selective independent review. Try a firstmate for the exercise and retain it only if useful. Secondmates and remote hardware depend on the task.

Jeffrey Emanuel

Write tasks another worker can pick up.

The Flywheel separates global planning from self-contained execution tasks, with durable records for coordination and memory. Beads Rust stores work records and dependencies; Beads Viewer adds graph-aware analysis. This curriculum applies those mechanisms with only the tooling needed for the exercise. [E1] [E2] [E3]

E1Describe uncertainty before delegating

Foundation: Specification, tradeoff analysis, and empirical discovery.

Practice: Write a short behavior model: user, constraints, invariants, unknowns, and acceptance evidence. Ask for a competing plan, then choose which uncertainty needs a prototype instead of more prose.

Mastery: Know when a stable compatibility target merits detailed planning and when a user hypothesis cannot be resolved by planning alone.

E2Create restartable work packets

Foundation: Task decomposition, dependency graphs, and interface design.

Practice: Break one real change into four to eight tasks. Each packet carries purpose, prerequisites, touch points, tests, and completion evidence. Give one packet to a fresh worker with no prior chat.

Mastery: Detect a missing dependency and a dependency that needlessly forces work to run in sequence. Judge independence by shared invariants as well as file boundaries.

E3Save context with evidence and provenance

Foundation: Documentation, provenance, cache invalidation, and knowledge retrieval.

Practice: Write a short current-state note with code and commit links, unresolved questions, and an owner. Resume from it in a fresh session. Deliberately include one stale claim and require the worker to inspect its source again.

Mastery: Separate authoritative current state, historical rationale, and an unverified memory. Do not promote a single successful workaround into universal policy.

E4Turn repeated friction into a small tool

Foundation: Automation, API design, and feedback economics.

Practice: Choose a repeated inspection or transformation. Make a thin, rerunnable command with clear inputs, useful errors, and safe cleanup. Compare it with the manual procedure once.

Mastery: Explain whether the tool saves work or shifts it into maintenance. Keep it limited to a reusable operation.

Plan in more detail as work approaches, and isolate execution. Use graph analysis to help schedule work; determine product priority separately. Judge correctness from results rather than plan size, bead count, or the volume of evidence files.

Steve Yegge

Keep responsibility and state clear across sessions.

Yegge describes persistent roles, deterministic monitors that invoke models for judgment, and a distinction between a durable seat and a temporary session. His handoff practices inform the lifecycle exercises here. This plan does not rely on his forecasts or claims about model welfare. [Y1] [Y2]

Y1Separate a role from a process

Foundation: Service identity, lifecycle management, and technical ownership.

Practice: Define a small operational charter: purpose, state, permissions, owner, and escalation. Run the same role in a fresh session using only its durable record. Start with release-note drafting or read-only triage.

Mastery: Explain what must survive a restart and what should expire. Persistent identity is not evidence that the new session is correct.

Y2Automate observation and use judgment for ambiguity

Foundation: Monitoring, event processing, and system boundaries.

Practice: Write a deterministic check that emits a compact event. Use an agent only to interpret an ambiguous result or propose a response; keep the actual write separately authorized.

Mastery: Show that idle monitoring costs no model calls. Explain false alarms, duplicate events, and the risk of blindly following hostile log text.

Y3Make handoff and recovery explicit

Foundation: Graceful shutdown, cancellation, leases, and fault handling.

Practice: Interrupt a bounded job after partial progress. Capture completed work, current revision, pending effects, and next safe action. Resume without duplicating the effect or erasing another owner’s work.

Mastery: Name the ambiguous state instead of guessing. Explain why a timeout is an observation, not proof of cancellation.

Y4Feed operational outcomes into better engineering

Foundation: Postmortems, backpressure, deployment safety, and organizational learning.

Practice: Inject a recoverable failure or integration conflict. Trace the failure, choose rollback or repair, and change one structural safeguard. Reduce incoming work if validation cannot keep up.

Mastery: Distinguish a local bug from an incentive created by the system. Preserve the incident record and keep any new rule proportionate to the failure.

Use one bounded role or a local simulation. A permanently broken main branch, unchecked production autonomy, unlimited tokens, or a simulated organization is not the default for these exercises.

Choose an approach by its assumptions

Learn when each approach is right.

The curriculum uses the following decision rules to compare the approaches. They are not positions all four practitioners endorse.

Tension Decision to practice Where
Detailed plan or executable prototype? Use Emanuel-style decomposition for a known contract or migration. Use Poteto-style sketches and experiments when the missing information is behavioral or empirical. Keep a short rationale for either choice. Modules 2, 3, 7
Durable task or persistent owner? Use a transferable packet for bounded work. For recurring operations, consider Chen's domain ownership or Yegge's role continuity. Before making ownership persistent, document the owner's authority and recovery procedure. Modules 1, 7
Prompt, memory, or hard constraint? Put current task context in the brief, rationale in a decision record, and reusable invariants in code or tooling. Retrieve historical material when relevant; do not inject the entire history into every session. Modules 3, 6, 7
One agent or multiple agents? First improve the tool and task interface. Add a worker for separate work, a reviewer for a different judgment, or an alternative for a consequential design. After the exercise, remove any added layer that was not useful. Modules 3, 5, 7
Prevent every defect or recover rapidly? Use checks and independent review appropriate to the risk before integration, then practice rollback or repair. If validation queues grow, consider batching, reducing scope, or limiting incoming work. A slow queue does not authorize breaking main. Modules 5, 7, 8

The approaches differ: Emanuel favors generalist workers and coordination through artifacts; Chen uses a liaison and supervisors; Yegge gives persistent roles institutional history; Poteto makes source structure and verification central. Understand these tradeoffs even if you use one agent plus a reviewer. [E1] [C1] [Y1] [P1]

03Modularity, domain types, and safe architectural changeDesign judgment · information hiding · refactoringW11–18 · 72 h0 / 3 mastery checks

Choose boundaries that simplify reasoning and make ordinary agent contributions less likely to damage the architecture.

Why study this: This is the highest-priority design block. Learn to spot missing domain concepts, excessive coupling, shallow wrappers, and invalid states before compensating for them with instructions and suppressions.

P1P3P4E2C2

Foundational concepts

Information hiding; cohesive responsibilities; deep versus shallow interfaces; data ownership; sum/product types; semantic identifiers; boundary parsing; explicit errors and effects; state machines; characterization tests; dependency direction; compatibility and migration strategies. “Fewer files” and “more abstractions” are neither universal goals.

Selected study, inside the weekly budget

Use A Philosophy of Software Design for complexity and module depth, then Fundamentals of Software Architecture for characteristics, coupling, and decision tradeoffs.

Practice independently

Find a boundary with duplicated checks or several interacting booleans. Write the legal and illegal states, then design two representations with caller examples before implementation. Explain their tradeoffs. Implement the core transformation yourself and capture current behavior in checks. Ask an agent to propose a plausible but unnecessary abstraction, then identify its cost.

Apply the skill with AI

Refactor the chosen boundary in the main product. Delegate caller migration and routine tests through explicit work packets, while you control the domain model and compatibility policy. Add one structural safeguard where it removes a recurring error. Review real comments and rationale; do not delete explanations just because a skill favors fewer comments.

Stop treating every model disagreement as a new requirement. Drop memory systems without a retrieval need and agent personas without operational responsibility. Prioritize independent performance in core skills over case-study polish.

Safety applies to training: use disposable environments and sanitized data for failure injection, untrusted-input tests, and concurrency drills. Obtain permission before working on organizational code or accounts. Keep real users, production data, costs, and publication authority outside experimental agent discretion.

Assess the result: can you reason through unfamiliar software, make consequential design choices, build an adequate feedback loop, delegate within clear boundaries, and explain when the tools are wrong? Use your engineering understanding to adapt the four practitioners' mechanisms as the tools change.
